# BerkeleyGW: An Exhaustive Review of the Many-Body Perturbation Theory (GW/BSE) Package

## 1. Overview

BerkeleyGW is an open-source, massively parallel software package for computing the **excited-state electronic and optical properties of materials** using ab initio many-body perturbation theory (MBPT). It implements two complementary formalisms:

- The **GW approximation** (Hedin's equations truncated at first order in the screened Coulomb interaction *W*), used to compute **quasiparticle (QP) energies** — i.e., corrected band structures, ionization potentials, electron affinities, and band/HOMO-LUMO gaps beyond DFT.
- The **Bethe–Salpeter equation (BSE)**, built on top of a GW calculation, used to compute **neutral excitations** (excitons) and optical absorption spectra that include electron–hole (e-h) interaction effects.

BerkeleyGW does **not** perform ground-state DFT itself. It is a **post-processing / higher-level theory code** that consumes mean-field wavefunctions, eigenvalues, and density/potential information generated by an external DFT (or other mean-field) code, and uses them as the starting point for the many-body perturbative corrections. This two-stage architecture (mean-field → MBPT) is central to how the package is used in practice.

The package is developed primarily by the research group of Steven G. Louie at UC Berkeley / Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, with contributions from a large international collaborator base, and is distributed under a BSD-style open-source license.

## 2. Relationship to Mean-Field (DFT) Codes

Because BerkeleyGW requires a mean-field starting point, it is built around **wrapper/interface utilities** that convert the wavefunctions, charge densities, and related quantities from various DFT codes into BerkeleyGW's internal `WFN`/`RHO`/`VXC` file formats.

**Supported mean-field codes** include:

- **Quantum ESPRESSO** — the most widely used interface today, via the `pw2bgw.x` converter bundled with the QE distribution; supports both norm-conserving and PAW/ultrasoft-derived workflows (with appropriate care), spin-orbit/spinor wavefunctions, and HDF5 I/O.
- **PARATEC** (PARAllel Total Energy Code) — one of the original codes BerkeleyGW was developed alongside; plane-wave pseudopotential DFT.
- **ABINIT** — plane-wave/PAW DFT code with a dedicated BerkeleyGW-format export.
- **PARSEC** — real-space grid DFT code (useful for finite systems/clusters without imposed periodicity artifacts).
- **Octopus** — real-space/real-time DFT code.
- **SIESTA** — localized numerical atomic-orbital DFT code.
- **RMG (Real-space Multigrid)** — real-space DFT code.
- **TBPW** — a simple internal tight-binding/plane-wave testing utility bundled with BerkeleyGW.

Regardless of the mean-field origin, the downstream MBPT machinery (`epsilon`, `sigma`, `kernel`, `absorption`) is agnostic to which DFT code produced the wavefunctions — it only requires the standardized `WFN` (and related `RHO`, `VXC`, `WFNq`) file formats, optionally in HDF5.

**Practical caveats when choosing a mean-field starting point:**
- Standard semilocal DFT (LDA/GGA) is usually an adequate starting point for one-shot (G₀W₀) perturbation theory, but there are notorious failure cases — e.g., bulk germanium is often predicted (near-)metallic at the DFT level, and small-gap or negative-gap semilocal DFT results can break the perturbative assumption underlying one-shot GW.
- Certain molecules (e.g., SiH₄) can have LUMO orbitals that are spuriously bound in semilocal DFT when they should be unbound (resonances), degrading the quality of the empty-state manifold used in GW.
- These issues are typically addressed by (a) choosing a different mean-field starting point/functional (hybrid, meta-GGA, DFT+U, etc. — supported since BerkeleyGW 3.0), or (b) performing self-consistency in the GW step (e.g., static COHSEX-based eigenvalue self-consistency, or full self-consistent GW).
- Users must ensure consistent pseudopotentials/basis truncation, sufficient unoccupied-state convergence, and matching k-point/G-vector conventions between the mean-field run and the BerkeleyGW input decks.

## 3. Theoretical Foundations

### 3.1 The GW Approximation
GW corrects DFT/Kohn–Sham eigenvalues by replacing the (approximate, static) exchange-correlation potential with an energy-dependent, non-local **self-energy** Σ = *iGW*, where *G* is the one-particle Green's function and *W* is the dynamically screened Coulomb interaction. BerkeleyGW implements this via the methodology of **Hybertsen and Louie**, historically using a **generalized plasmon-pole (GPP) model** to avoid explicit real-frequency integration of *W*, though full-frequency (contour-deformation and real-axis) approaches are also supported in modern versions for systems where GPP is less reliable (e.g., metals, strongly correlated systems, or cases needing higher accuracy).

Key computational stages:
1. **`epsilon`**: Computes the random-phase-approximation (RPA) polarizability and inverts it to obtain the microscopic dielectric matrix ε(**q**,**G**,**G**′) (and its inverse), which encodes the screening of the Coulomb interaction.
2. **`sigma`**: Uses the dielectric matrix together with the mean-field wavefunctions, density, and exchange-correlation potential to evaluate the GW self-energy (split into a bare-exchange "Σx" term and a correlation "Σc" term), producing quasiparticle energy corrections, QP band structures, and QP lifetimes (from the imaginary part of Σ).

### 3.2 The Bethe–Salpeter Equation (BSE)
Optical absorption requires going beyond independent-quasiparticle transitions to account for the attractive electron–hole interaction that binds excitons. BerkeleyGW solves the BSE following the formalism of **Rohlfing and Louie**, typically within the **Tamm–Dancoff approximation**, using:
1. **`kernel`**: Computes the electron–hole interaction kernel (direct + exchange terms) on a coarse k-point grid, using the screened interaction *W* from `epsilon` and the mean-field wavefunctions.
2. **`absorption`**: Interpolates the coarse-grid kernel matrix elements onto a finer k-point grid (necessary because exciton binding requires much denser k-sampling than is computationally feasible for the kernel step itself), constructs and diagonalizes the effective BSE Hamiltonian (via direct diagonalization with LAPACK/ScaLAPACK or an iterative Haydock/Lanczos approach), and outputs exciton binding energies, eigenvectors, and the macroscopic optical absorption spectrum (with or without electron–hole interactions, for comparison to RPA).

### 3.3 Extended Capabilities (Later Versions)
- Full two-component **spinor wavefunctions** for strong spin–orbit coupling (SOC) effects in GW and GW-BSE.
- **Finite center-of-mass momentum** exciton states (exciton band structure, relevant for momentum-dark excitons and phonon-assisted processes).
- Broader mean-field starting points: LDA, GGA, hybrid functionals, meta-GGA, DFT+U.
- **`parabands`**: a companion utility for efficient generation of large numbers of unoccupied (empty) bands needed to converge GW sums, often more efficient than doing so directly in the mean-field code.
- Non-linear optics and real-time/plotting utilities (`nonlinearoptics`, `plotxct`, `visual`) for exciton wavefunction visualization and related properties.
- Electron–phonon coupling extensions and interfaces to downstream transport/dynamics codes.

## 4. Code Architecture and Workflow

A standard BerkeleyGW calculation follows this pipeline:

1. **Mean-field step** (external code: Quantum ESPRESSO, PARATEC, ABINIT, etc.) → produces `WFN` (and `WFNq`, `RHO`, `VXC` as needed) files, typically via a dedicated converter/wrapper.
2. **`epsilon`** → computes the RPA dielectric matrix, output as `eps0mat`/`epsmat` (or HDF5 equivalents).
3. **`sigma`** → computes GW quasiparticle self-energy corrections and QP energies (`eqp.dat`, QP band structure).
4. **`kernel`** → computes the BSE electron–hole interaction kernel matrix elements on a coarse grid (`bsemat.h5` / `bsedmat`+`bsexmat`).
5. **`absorption`** → interpolates onto the fine grid, diagonalizes the BSE Hamiltonian, and produces exciton energies/wavefunctions and the optical absorption spectrum.

Supporting utilities include mean-field wrapper converters, `parabands` (empty-state generation), and post-processing/visualization tools (`plotxct`, `visual`, `nonlinearoptics`, and the community-contributed `dbaAutomator` for double-Bader exciton-character analysis and fine-grid convergence checks in molecular crystals).

## 5. Performance and Scalability

BerkeleyGW is written predominantly in **Fortran90**, parallelized with **MPI + OpenMP**, and has demonstrated scaling to very large HPC systems:

- Historically scaled to **hundreds of thousands of CPU cores** (e.g., half a million cores on NERSC's Cori Phase 2 / Xeon-Phi system).
- Since roughly version 3.x/4.0, the package has gained **GPU acceleration** (initially targeting NVIDIA via CUDA/OpenACC-style approaches, and — in the 4.0 release — a fuller port using portable programming models supporting **NVIDIA, AMD, and Intel GPUs** across the whole GW/GW-BSE workflow).
- A widely cited benchmark computed GW quasiparticle excitations for divacancy defects in Si/SiC using a supercell of **> 2,700 atoms (>10,000 electrons)**, reaching **105.9 double-precision PFLOP/s (52.7% of peak)** running at full scale on **Summit at OLCF (27,648 GPUs)**.
- Stochastic and pseudoband-based methods have been introduced to accelerate convergence with respect to the number of empty (unoccupied) states, historically one of the dominant computational bottlenecks of GW.

This makes BerkeleyGW one of the few GW/BSE implementations explicitly engineered for leadership-class exascale/pre-exascale supercomputers, distinguishing it from many GW codes historically limited to systems of tens of atoms and modest core counts.

## 6. Applicability

BerkeleyGW supports:
- **Dimensionality**: 3D bulk crystals, 2D materials/slabs, 1D systems (nanotubes, nanowires), and 0D finite systems (molecules, clusters), using appropriate Coulomb-truncation schemes to remove spurious periodic-image interactions in reduced dimensions.
- **Electronic character**: insulators, semiconductors, metals, and semi-metals (metallic screening requires care, e.g., in the treatment of intraband/Drude-like contributions to ε).
- **System size**: from small molecules to multi-thousand-atom supercells (defects, disordered/amorphous systems, large 2D moiré structures) on large HPC allocations.

## 10. Summary

BerkeleyGW is a mature, actively developed, highly scalable open-source implementation of ab initio GW and GW-BSE many-body perturbation theory. Its defining architectural feature is a clean separation between the mean-field step (delegated to established DFT codes such as Quantum ESPRESSO, PARATEC, ABINIT, PARSEC, Octopus, SIESTA, and RMG) and the many-body perturbative step (handled internally by its four core executables: `epsilon`, `sigma`, `kernel`, and `absorption`). This design has made it one of the most widely used codes for computing quasiparticle band structures and excitonic optical spectra across bulk, low-dimensional, and molecular systems, with a strong track record of scaling to leadership-class HPC systems and, in its most recent releases, portable GPU acceleration across major GPU vendors.
